The people behind the pipes

Meet the pipers behind Mountain Piper.

Two Asheville musicians, one shared devotion: traditional Scottish bagpiping and the interceltic music that grew around it — performed at life’s biggest moments and passed on to new students across Western North Carolina.

Performer · Teacher · Competitor

Kit Rashid

Kit Rashid is proud to call Asheville, North Carolina, his home. Hailing originally from Rome, New York, he has 43 years of experience as a performer, teacher, and competitor on the Highland bagpipe.

He competes as a soloist in the professional grade of the Eastern United States Pipe Band Association — the highest competitive level — and serves as an instructor for the Grandfather Mountain Highlanders, the host band of the Grandfather Mountain Highland Games.

For over forty years, Kit has provided music for an astonishing range of occasions: weddings, funerals, Kirkin’ o’ the Tartans services, college commencements, collegiate sporting events, festivals, regattas, and horse races — any gathering where the pipes can do what they do best.

He is an equally experienced teacher, at home with private students and full groups alike. Put simply, piping is his vocation — and he is firmly committed to sharing the music he loves in a way that is completely inclusive and always professional.

Performer · Teacher

Alex Stewart

Alex Stewart is an Asheville-based piper performing traditional Scottish bagpiping and interceltic music with Mountain Piper.

On stage and off, Alex carries the same standard that defines the Mountain Piper name: music prepared with care and played with heart, for weddings, memorials, festivals, and ceremonies across Western North Carolina — and a genuine love of teaching the tradition to those just finding their way to it.

Together, Kit and Alex give Mountain Piper something rare: the depth to honor a quiet funeral with the same artistry they bring to a festival crowd, and the bench strength to be there when your date matters most.

The music itself

Scottish piping, and the interceltic world around it.

The Great Highland bagpipe

The instrument most people picture when they hear “bagpipes”: three drones over the shoulder, nine notes on the chanter, and a voice built for open ground. Its music — marches, strathspeys, reels, jigs, hornpipes, and the great slow airs — is one of the world’s oldest continuous performance traditions.

Interceltic music

The Celtic nations — Scotland, Ireland, Brittany, Wales, Cornwall, the Isle of Man, and beyond — have traded tunes for centuries. Interceltic music celebrates that shared heritage, and it gives Mountain Piper a repertoire wide enough to fit almost any occasion and any family’s roots.
